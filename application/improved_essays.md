## 1 Why ASDRP (limit 375), 280 words

Anesthesia works by changing how neurons talk to each other, and that is where my interest in neuroscience started. I plan to become a Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetist, and I want to understand what is happening at the cellular level when a patient goes under: how neurons interact, how circuits respond to different stimuli, and how diseases or drugs change the way those signals travel.

Right now I explore these questions through code. Combining computer science with biology lets me take on problems too complicated to solve by hand, and I would rather ask my own questions of data than memorize what someone else has already found. I have used public datasets to study sleep, Alzheimer's brain scans, and activity in mouse visual cortex, where I tested whether a mouse's pupil size and running help predict what its neurons do. The result held across three mice, and checking whether it could be an accident taught me more than the result itself.

I am applying to ASDRP because I have taken my independent work about as far as I can alone. I want mentors who can show me how to analyze data more carefully, question my own results, and explain what I find. I also want to be around students who push me into topics I would not have looked at on my own.

Longer term, I want to pair neuroscience with healthcare through college and beyond. Understanding the science of anesthesia at the cellular level should help me make better decisions for patients once I am practicing. ASDRP is where I want to build the habits to work at that level, in the lab now and in the hospital someday.

## 2 Coursework and grades (limit 400), 252 words

In ninth grade I took Biology and Precalculus, the hardest science and math courses available to me.

Biology is where my interest in neuroscience and medicine began. Learning how genetics, cells, and the body's regulation work made me want to know what happens at that same level in the brain. I earned an A, and the class is a large part of why I am drawn to research.

Precalculus was harder, and I ended the year with a high B, the toughest grade I have worked for. The course moved fast, with no extra credit or test corrections, and I realized early that keeping up took more than attending lectures. I did extra problems, went back through my notes, and stayed with the topics that gave me trouble until I could use them. By the end I understood the material in a way I would not have if it had come easily.

This year I am taking Honors Chemistry and AP Calculus AB. Chemistry sits behind most of what I care about, from how neurons signal to how medications act on the body. Calculus builds directly on Precalculus and is the math I need for the quantitative questions I want to take on in research.

My classes have given me a foundation in math and biology. The more useful lesson has been what to do when I get stuck and how to learn something without waiting for someone to explain it again. Those are the habits I want to bring to ASDRP.

## 3 Other STEM experience (limit 375), 333 words

Outside the classroom, I look for activities that build both my scientific curiosity and my ability to help others.

I tutor math with Learn To Be. One student on IXL could handle the arithmetic but froze at any problem longer than a sentence. We spent sessions breaking problems into pieces and underlining what each part was asking before touching any numbers. Once she saw it was mostly translation, her scores climbed. Tutoring has taught me to slow down, listen for where someone is actually confused, and try explanations until one lands. I understand math differently after teaching it.

I also volunteer with Pragnya, which supports neurodivergent children. I run games and activities and adjust how I interact with each child. One girl I was paired with still remembers my name on days I am not with her, comes over to say hi, and starts conversations about shared interests. Watching how differently each child engages with the same activity was my first close look at how the brain shapes behavior, and a big part of why I want to study neuroscience.

That interest carries into projects I do on my own. Using Python and public datasets, I explore sleep, EEG signals, neuronal activity, and Alzheimer's. For one project I built a classifier on a brain imaging dataset to tell healthy scans from scans at different stages of Alzheimer's. Getting the preprocessing right was harder than the modeling, and it showed me how much of research happens in the careful steps before any result. Alzheimer's interests me because of stories I have read and a film about families living with it. I noticed not only what the disease does to a person, but how much the people around them carry.

I want to bring these habits to ASDRP and do research seriously, alongside mentors who can push my thinking further than I can alone. Longer term, I want to use computation to study neurodegenerative disease, applying data and modeling to questions that matter for care.

## 4 Diversity (limit 200), 168 words

Volunteering at Pragnya has shown me how differently each child engages with the same room. In one session, one child was dancing while another covered their ears because the noise was too much. Some of the kids I work with speak in single words. Others are non-verbal and communicate in other ways. Each has their own ideas about what to play and how, and most are more creative than mine.

That is what diversity looks like to me: real, in front of me, in how people move through a room. It has made me a better listener. The game I planned is often not the game we end up playing, and the child usually knows better than I do what will work.

I want to be part of a research group that works the same way, where people arrive with their own approach and are not asked to smooth it out. The person doing something differently is often the one seeing what the rest of us are missing.
