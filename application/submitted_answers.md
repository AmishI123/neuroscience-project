## 1. Why ASDRP (limit 375)
My interest is in molecular neuroscience, and my plan is to become a Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetist. Anesthesia acts directly on neurons and the signals between them, so understanding how the brain works at the cellular level matters both for what I find interesting and for the career I want. I am curious about how neurons interact, how circuits respond to different stimuli, and how diseases or drugs change the way those signals travel.

The way I explore these questions right now is through code. Combining computer science with biology lets me work on problems that are too complicated to solve by hand, and I would rather ask my own questions of data than memorize what someone else has already found.

I am applying to ASDRP because I have taken my independent work about as far as I can alone. I would like to work with mentors who can guide me on how to analyze data more carefully, question my own results, and explain what I find. I also want to be surrounded by students who push me to go deeper into topics I would not have looked at on my own.

Longer term, I want to keep pairing neuroscience with healthcare through college and beyond. Understanding the science behind anesthesia at the cellular level should help me make better decisions for patients once I am practicing. ASDRP is where I want to build the experience and habits to work at that level, both in the lab and eventually in the hospital.

## 2. Coursework and grades (limit 400)
In ninth grade I took Biology and Precalculus, the hardest science and math courses available to me.

Biology was where my interest in neuroscience and medicine started. Learning about genetics, cells, and how the body regulates itself made me want to know what happens at that same level in the brain. I earned an A in the class, and it is a large part of why I ended up drawn to research.

Precalculus was harder. I ended the year with a high B, which was the toughest grade I had worked for. The class moved fast, with no extra credit or test corrections, and I realized early on that keeping up would take more than showing up to lectures. I picked up the work on my own, doing extra problems, going back through my notes, and staying with the topics that gave me trouble until I could actually use them. By the end of the course I understood the material in a way I would not have if it had come easily.

This year I am taking Honors Chemistry and AP Calculus AB. I am looking forward to chemistry because it sits behind most of what I care about, from how neurons signal to how medications act on the body. Calculus builds directly on the work I did in Precalculus, and it is the math I need for the kinds of quantitative questions I want to take on in research.

My classes have given me a foundation in math and biology, but the more useful thing they have taught me is what to do when I get stuck, and how to learn something without waiting for someone to explain it again. Those are the habits I want to bring to ASDRP.

## 3. Other STEM experience (limit 375; check the form's counter, this may be over)
Outside the classroom, I look for activities that build both my scientific curiosity and my ability to help others.

One of the most meaningful activities is tutoring math with Learn To Be. One student on IXL could handle the arithmetic but froze at any problem with more than one sentence. We spent sessions breaking problems into pieces and underlining what each was asking before touching numbers. Once she saw it was mostly translation, her scores climbed. Working with students like her has pushed me to slow down, listen for where someone is actually confused, and try a few explanations until one lands. I understand math differently after teaching it.

I volunteer with Pragnya, which supports neurodivergent children. I run games and activities and adjust how I interact based on each child's needs. One girl I was paired with a while back is someone I still think about. We got along well from the first session, and on days I am not working with her, she remembers my name, comes up to say hi, starts conversations about similar interests, and shows small signs of affection. Watching how differently each child engages with the same activity has been my first close look at how the brain shapes behavior, and a big part of why I want to study neuroscience.

That interest carries into projects I do on my own. Using Python and public datasets, I explore questions around sleep, EEG signals, neuronal activity, and Alzheimer's. In one project, I worked with a brain imaging dataset from Kaggle and built a classifier to distinguish healthy scans from scans at different stages of Alzheimer's. Getting the preprocessing right was harder than the modeling, and it taught me how much of research is in the careful steps before any result. Alzheimer's interests me because of stories I have read and a film about families living with it. What I noticed was not only what the disease does to the person, but how much those around them carry too.

I want to bring these habits to ASDRP and do research seriously, alongside mentors who can push my thinking further than I can on my own. Longer term, I want to use computation to study neurodegenerative disease, applying data and modeling to questions that matter for care.

## 4. Diversity (limit 200)
Volunteering at Pragnya has shown me how differently each kid engages with the same room. In one session I watched one child dancing while another covered their ears because the noise was too much. Some of the kids I work with speak in single words. Others are non-verbal and communicate in other ways. Every one of them has their own ideas about what to play and how to play it, and most of those ideas are more creative than what I would have come up with.

That is what diversity looks like to me. Real, in front of me, in how people move through a room. Being around it has made me a better listener, because I have learned that the game I planned is often not the game we end up playing, and the child usually knows better than I do what will actually work.

I want to be part of a research group that works the same way, where people arrive with their own approach and are not asked to smooth it out. The person doing something differently is often the one seeing what the rest of us are missing.
