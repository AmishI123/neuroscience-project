When I learned that the *same* image can make a neuron in the visual cortex fire
differently depending on whether an animal is calm or alert, I couldn't stop thinking
about it. The brain isn't a camera. What we perceive depends on what's happening
inside us. That idea — that internal state shapes perception — is what pulls me
toward neuroscience, and it's why I want to spend real time *doing* research instead
of only reading about it.

Over the past weeks I've been teaching myself to test that idea with actual data.
Using recordings from thousands of neurons in mouse visual cortex, I built models that
predict neural activity from the images the mice saw, then asked whether adding their
behavior — arousal and movement — improved those predictions. It did in both animals:
the original mouse rose from 0.126 to 0.158, and a second mouse rose from 0.154 to
0.203. Pupil-linked arousal helped both, while running contributed differently across
animals. The replication made the result more interesting, not less: it showed me that
a pattern can repeat while its components still vary. The data corrected my assumptions,
and that surprise was the part I loved most.

That experience taught me that I want research, not just results — the slow, careful
work of asking a question, being wrong, and following what the data actually says.
